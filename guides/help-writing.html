Help writing guide

Words guide. Edit this guide on GitHub.

Version 1.4, 17 November 2026. How help is written for every help centre (Starboard, Snaglist, the plug-in collection and any later one) and any help shown inside the apps. Section 11 is the help centre standard: the layout, the menus, flow charts, the journey, the problem solver and plug-in mode. Every word also follows CONTENT-DESIGN.md.

About this guide

What it is for

Help answers the question someone has at the moment they have it. This guide sets out how that help is organised and laid out. Every app's help centre then works in the same way, with the same three tiers, kinds of topic, layouts and checks. Someone who has used one help centre already knows how the next one is organised.

Readers may know very little about computers. Write so that someone who has never opened a terminal can follow every step, and link to the basics for anything they might not know.

Who it is for

Anyone who writes or checks help: the help centre in an app's repository, help shown inside an app, and README files that teach. That includes AI assistants: section 9 says what to give one and how to check what it writes. It is public, so you may also use it for your own projects under the guide licence (CC BY 4.0).

How to use it

  1. Choose the tier (section 1): a topic, a guide or the basics.
  2. For a topic, choose its one kind (section 2) and the area it belongs to (section 3).
  3. Copy the layout for that tier (sections 4 to 6) and write to the rules in section 7.
  4. Add screenshots only where section 8 says they help.
  5. Before you finish, go through the checklist in section 10 and the one in CONTENT-DESIGN.md.

Working examples: the Starboard help centre and the Snaglist help centre. The gallery, the tools and the other guides are listed in Design resources and tools.

1. The three tiers

Tier Folder What it is Answers
1 topics/ A topic: one task, one question, one article "How do I do this one thing?"
2 guides/ A guide: an ordered journey made of topics "How do I get from nothing to this outcome?"
3 basics/ The basics: an idea explained from the start "What is this, and why does it matter?"
  • A topic covers one task. A second task is a second topic, and the two link to each other.
  • A guide is mostly links. Each step is one or two sentences and a link to the topic that holds the instructions. A guide never repeats a topic's steps, so each instruction is kept in one place. If a step has no topic, write the topic first.
  • Basics teach, topics and guides direct. If a topic needs to explain what Docker is before it can give the steps, link to the basics article instead.

2. Kinds of topic

Every topic is exactly one of these. A topic that needs two kinds is two topics.

Kind Use it for Title starts with
How-to Doing one task A verb: "Add an app"
Reference Facts to check: settings, limits, lists The thing: "Display and accessibility settings"
Troubleshooting One problem and its fix The problem in the reader's words: "Starboard does not open"
Overview A hub that routes onwards within one area The area: "Backup and restore"

3. Areas and groups

Every help centre has the same seven main items. In order, they are Home, Get started, Use it, Plug-ins, Accessibility, Fix a problem and Developers (section 11). Each of the six areas holds a few groups, named in the reader's words. Starboard's Use it has Dashboard, Apps, Library and downloads, Your server, Alerts, Security, Backups and Updates.

Every article sits in exactly one group. An article that fits two groups belongs to the one the reader would try first. An area with nothing in it says so in one line, such as "Snaglist has no plug-ins".

  • Get started: before installing, installing, opening it on other devices, moving in.
  • Use it: the app's own areas, in the order of its main menu, then settings, backups and updates.
  • Plug-ins: adding and managing plug-ins; in a plug-in collection, every plug-in (section 11.7).
  • Accessibility: the display settings, ways to use it, how it is tested and its limits.
  • Fix a problem: troubleshooting topics, each one flow chart first, and the problem solver.
  • Developers: plug-in writing, the API, prompts for AI assistants, security and tests.

The tiers (section 1) still decide the folder and the layout; the area and group decide where an article is listed.

4. Topic layout (tier 1)

[Help centre](../README.md) · <Area>

# <The task, in the reader's words>

<Kind: how-to, reference or troubleshooting>

One or two sentences: what this does and what you will have at the end. The answer comes first.

## Before you begin          (omit when there is nothing)
- What must be true or to hand, each with a link to the topic that sets it up.

## When would you use it     (reference topics only)
- Real situations, each starting "You want to…" or "You need to…".

## Steps
1. One action per step, with the on-screen label in bold: Select **Add an app**.
2. Commands in a fenced `bash` block of their own, one command per block.
3. Say what happens after a step when it is not obvious: "Starboard shows the setup code."

## Things to note            (omit when there is nothing)
## If it does not work       (omit when there is nothing)
## Find out more
- Links to related topics, guides and basics, and to the topic's parent.

Empty sections are left out, never padded. "Find out more" points onwards; it never sums up the topic.

5. Guide layout (tier 2)

[Help centre](../README.md) · Guides

# <The outcome, in the reader's words>

Who this is for, what you will have at the end, and about how long it takes.

## Before you begin
## The steps at a glance       (a numbered list, then a flow chart of the same steps)
## Step 1: <short name>        (one or two sentences, the link to the topic, and how you know it worked)
## What next

Name a guide after the goal ("Move from Plex to Jellyfin"), not after a feature. State what each step depends on, so the order is clear. The flow chart (section 11.4) is drawn with the same steps written out in words.

6. Basics layout (tier 3)

[Help centre](../README.md) · The basics

# <What X is>

The idea in two or three sentences, with an everyday comparison where one helps.

## <A few short sections that build the idea up>
## What this means for <app>
## Find out more

7. Rules for help

  1. Titles in the reader's words, with the word they would search for: "Set up two-step sign-in with an authenticator app".
  2. Other names once, near the top: "two-step sign-in, also called 2FA or two-factor authentication". Search and screen readers both use them.
  3. Every child links to its parent, and each topic links to its nearest neighbours under "Find out more".
  4. Commands are copied, not entered by hand. Put each command in its own bash block, and say what it does in the sentence before it. Mark anything the reader must change, such as <your server>, and say what to put there.

Some people enter them by hand anyway. A command with a symbol that moves between keyboards (|, ~, \, ", @, $, &) links to the app's keyboard help. That help covers US, UK and Ireland, German, French and Spanish keyboards. A one-line command with | also shows a version without it.

On UK keyboards the ¦ key is not |, so say so. 5. One action per step, starting with the verb. Start from a page the reader can find, and give the full path the first time (CONTENT-DESIGN.md rule 2.19). 6. Callouts by meaning (CONTENT-DESIGN.md section 7): "Before you begin:", "Limitation:", "Warning:", "Tip:", "Example:". Never a callout without its lead word.

Default set-up first, more options apart

Owner, 15 November 2026 (Starboard DASH-439): help shows the default set-up first, with additional apps and more options apart from it.

  1. Split by need, not by person. A guide walks a new person through one path with no choices. A how-to assumes some context and does one task. A reference lists every setting.

A basics article says why. Do not mix them. Explanation and lists of options do not belong inside a guide's steps. 2. Name sections by what they hold, never by who the reader is. Write "The default set-up", "Add more apps" and "More options", not "For beginners" or "For experts". People misjudge themselves, and the line is blurred. 3. Default path first. The main steps use the defaults as they are. A choice a new person can skip says so in one line ("You can change this later in More options") and links to it. Additional apps and more options come after the main steps, under their own headings or, when short, in a disclosure (details). The test: a new person finishes the main task without opening or reading anything after the main steps. 4. Signpost at the top: one line saying what the page covers (such as the default install) and where the rest is. 5. One plain-language baseline. Sections of more options follow the same writing rules; they are separate, not harder to read. 6. Navigation: Get started holds only the default path. More options and additional topics are found through the side menu, search and links, and Developers stays its own area.

8. Screenshots

The full rules, for people and AI tools, are in SCREENSHOTS.md, with a mark-up helper in tools/annotate.js. In short:

  • Add one only for a step the reader could not follow from the words. There is no quota.
  • Everything a screenshot shows is also said in words (rule 2.20). Text descriptions follow ALT-TEXT.md.
  • Take them from a preview with made-up data (rule 2.23), never from a real server; never fake one.
  • Marking up: a red outline 5 pixels outside the control, and a curved arrow whose filled head touches it. Use at most three outlines, each with a badge carrying its step's number in the article.
  • Alt text names the page and the control by its label: "The Apps page, with Add an app outlined". Never "the button in the bottom right".

9. Getting AI to write instructions

An AI assistant can draft help, release notes and on-screen words well, but only from facts it is given. Left alone it fills gaps with what sounds likely: labels that do not exist, steps in the wrong order, and the words this guide bans. Treat its draft as a first draft that you check.

What to give it

  • This guide and CONTENT-DESIGN.md, to read in full before it writes anything.
  • The facts: the app's code or its running pages, so labels come from the app, not from memory. For a change, the commit or the release notes behind it.
  • The reader and the job: who it is for, which tier (topic, guide or basics) and which kind of topic.
  • What already exists: the help home and the nearest topics. The agent links to them instead of repeating them, and does not write a second topic for the same task.

What it must do

  1. Read the rules first, then say which tier and kind it is writing.
  2. Copy every on-screen label exactly from the code or the running app, in bold inside steps.
  3. Never invent behaviour, labels, limits or permissions. Where it is unsure, it writes [needs checking: …] in the draft and carries on, rather than guessing.
  4. Use made-up example data only (CONTENT-DESIGN.md rule 2.23).
  5. Link, do not repeat. A step that has its own topic links to it. A missing topic is reported as a gap, not written inline.
  6. Change only what was asked. When editing, keep the article's existing sections and links, and do not rewrite the voice while fixing one fact.
  7. Report back: what it wrote and every [needs checking]. Also any label it left as it is because the app shows it that way, and anything wrong it noticed but did not fix.

How to check its work

Check How
Banned words and punctuation Run The sweep, in this section. Fix every hit, or confirm it is an on-screen label. "See" is the one that slips through most, inside friendly sentences ("so you can see what is left").
Labels Open the page in the app and compare every bold label, letter for letter.
Steps Follow the steps on a test install or a preview, from the starting page the topic names. A step you cannot follow is wrong.
Facts Every number, limit, default and version matches the code or the release notes. Clear every [needs checking].
Links Every link opens the right article; the parent and related articles are linked.
Structure Right tier, one kind, headings in order, empty sections left out (section 10 checklist).
Privacy tools/check-private.sh passes: no real names, addresses, keys or server names.

The sweep, run on the draft (it lists lines to read, not lines that are always wrong):

grep -nE "—|\b(can't|don't|won't|it's|isn't|doesn't|you're|we're|what's|that's|you'll)\b" draft.md
grep -niE "\b(click\w*|tap\w*|see[ns]?|seeing|look\w*|enable[ds]?|disable[ds]?|toggle\w*|turn(s|ed)? (on|off)|screen|type[ds]?|typing|please|drives?)\b|\bOn:" draft.md
grep -niE "\b(simple|simply|easy|just|straightforward|obviously|complex|difficult|advanced)\b" draft.md
grep -niE "\b(above|below|top right|on the left|on the right|scroll down|click here|read more|learn more)\b|!" draft.md

The design system runs the same words as a check on its own guides, gallery, boards and terminal frame. python3 tools/check_words.py stops on a hit. A genuine exception goes in tools/words-allowed.txt with its reason.

A tool reporting success is not the check: read the result back, in the file and on the page.

A prompt to start with

Write a help topic for <app>: "<task in the reader's words>".
First read docs/HELP-WRITING.md and docs/CONTENT-DESIGN.md in the design system, in full.
Take every on-screen label from <path to the page's code, or the running app>; copy them exactly.
Never invent behaviour, labels or limits: write [needs checking: ...] instead and carry on.
Use made-up example data only. Link to existing topics in <help folder> instead of repeating them.
When you finish, run the sweep in HELP-WRITING.md section 9 on your draft and fix every hit.
Then tell me: the tier and kind, every [needs checking], labels left as the app shows them, and any gaps.

10. Checklist before a help change is finished

  • [ ] The article is in the right tier, and a topic has exactly one kind
  • [ ] Title in the reader's words; other names given once near the top
  • [ ] The default path comes first; additional apps and more options are in their own sections (section 7, Default set-up first, more options apart)
  • [ ] The answer comes first; empty sections left out
  • [ ] A guide links to topics instead of repeating their steps
  • [ ] Links to the parent and to related articles under "Find out more"
  • [ ] Screenshots only where needed, described in words, with made-up data, marked up to SCREENSHOTS.md
  • [ ] The article has its <!-- help: <area> / <group> --> line, and the group is in help-centre.json
  • [ ] A troubleshooting topic or a guide starts with its flow chart; every numbered answer has its section
  • [ ] The help centre builds with no warnings (build.py --strict)
  • [ ] Everything in the CONTENT-DESIGN.md checklist

11. The help centre standard

Chosen by the owner on 14 November 2026 (Starboard DASH-436): "I also want this design to become the standard. This is to be used in all help centres." The design system's builder, tools/help_centre/build.py, builds every help centre. It reads the Markdown in the app's repository and one help-centre.json. Nothing here is drawn by hand for each build.

Change the Markdown or the settings, and build again. The pieces are components in components.css and components.js, shown in the gallery, so an app can use them too.

11.1 Layout

  • Top bar: the help centre's name, then the seven main items as quick links (.top-links), then Search and Display. The area you are in is marked. On tablets and phones the seven items move into the side menu and the bar keeps Menu, Search and Display.
  • A hub on every page: the side menu on the left, the page on the right. Articles keep On this page beside them on wide screens, inside them on narrow ones.
  • Area pages: each of the six areas has a page with a card for each group, listing every article in it.
  • Home: the welcome heading with the latest version beside it on the same line, with no date. The version (.version-badge) is read from the changelog or a version file, never entered by hand. Then come the journey, the newest thing (.feature-card) and the problem solver card (.solver-card), which is always under the newest thing.

11.2 The side menu

A side menu that folds (.side-tree) lists Home, then each area, then the groups in it, then every article in each group. - Areas and groups are details and summary, so they work with no script and a screen reader hears each one as expanded or collapsed. Each group shows how many articles it holds. - The page you are on is marked (aria-current="page"), and its area and group are open. The rest are shut. - Each area starts with Overview, its area page. Fix a problem also lists the Problem solver. - On tablets and phones the menu opens from Menu over the page. The area you are in is already open, and the focus is on the page you are on. The page behind cannot be reached while the menu is open. Escape, Menu again or choosing a page closes it, and the focus goes back to Menu. - With no script, the menu comes before the page.

Each article says where it belongs, on the line after its trail line:

[Help centre](../README.md) · When something is wrong · troubleshooting
<!-- help: fix / starboard -->

The area is one of start, use, plugins, a11y, fix and dev; the group is a key from help-centre.json. An article with no line, or an unknown group, is listed under Other and the build warns; --strict makes that an error. Within a group, articles keep the order of the help home's lists, then the order of their titles.

11.3 The journey

The home page starts with the journey (.journey). It has three to five stages, left to right on a dashed path. Each stage has a column of two or three cards under it (.stage-card) that link to the articles for that stage. The first card in each column is the one to start with.

The first stage is the start, and the last is fixing things. On phones the stages stack with the path down the side. A plug-in collection's home has its own journey (choose, add, use, update or remove). Keep each card to a title and one line.

11.4 Flow charts

Every troubleshooting topic starts with a flow chart (articles are flow chart first), and so does every guide (its steps at a glance). A how-to with a real choice in it (which version, which kind of disk) may have one. A reference topic or a basics article does not need one. Put the chart straight after the opening sentences.

Write the chart in a fenced flow block. It is plain to read on GitHub. The builder draws it as an SVG that follows Light, Dark and High contrast. It then writes the same paths out in words under the chart (The chart in words), as the full text equivalent:

```flow
title: Find your answer
start: An app's card says Not responding
step: Open the app's details | Select its name on Apps
q: What do the details say?
  Cannot reach the app -> q2
  Refused the API key -> 3
  Answered with an error -> 4
q2: Does the app open in your browser?
  No -> 1
  Yes -> 2
1: Start the app again #start-the-app-again
2: Check the address Starboard uses #check-the-address-starboard-uses
3: Copy the API key again #copy-the-api-key-again
4: Read the app's own messages #read-the-apps-own-messages
```
  • One node a line:
  • start: (exactly one, first)
  • q: or q<name>: for a question (at least two answers)
  • step<name>: for something that happens
  • end<name>: for a good end
  • warn<name>: for the last thing to try
  • a number for a numbered answer

| words adds a short second line. - Answers are indented under the node they leave from: <what the reader sees or chooses> -> <target>. The target is a question, a number, a step or end, or another article (check-disk-health.md). A start or a step with no answers leads to the next line. - Each numbered answer is a section of the article. Give its heading with #its-id at the end of the line, or use the heading that starts with the number ("1. Start the app again"). The builder shows each as an answer card (.answer-card), with the number and heading on a grey band and the steps under it.

Using #its-id keeps the heading's words and its address, so links to it from release notes still work. - An answer reached from another answer ("Still does not open -> 5") is drawn as the next thing to try. - Keep questions short (under eight words), answers to what is on the reader's page, and a chart to about ten nodes. A chart that will not fit is two articles. - Plain Mermaid flowcharts (flowchart TD with A[...] --> B{...}) are drawn the same way, so older guides need no change; write new charts as flow blocks. No chart library is loaded in the browser.

11.5 The problem solver

solver.html asks up to four questions and takes the reader to the article that fixes the problem. It is written in help-centre.json under solver as a tree. Each answer has to (an article) or next (the next question). With no script, it is the whole tree as lists of links.

With script, it asks one question at a time, with Back and Start again. It puts the focus on each new question and says it politely. Every Fix a problem article should be reachable from it. Its card (.solver-card) goes on the home page under the newest thing, and at the top of Fix a problem.

11.6 Pages

  • Articles: breadcrumbs (help centre, area, group, title), the kind, the article, On this page, and a link to the Markdown on GitHub.
  • Search: every article, searched in the browser; every word must appear, titles count most.
  • Display and accessibility: the same choices as the apps, applied before the page draws.

11.7 Plug-in mode

A help centre for a plug-in collection adds plugins to help-centre.json. Its Plug-ins area becomes the plug-ins themselves. Each group of plug-ins is an area in the side menu, and each plug-in is a folding item in it. Under every plug-in are the same eight pages in the same order: Overview, Install, Settings, Dashboard card, Updates, Accessibility, Problems and For developers.

Overview is the plug-in's own README, and the others are help/plugins/<id>/<page>.md. A page with nothing to say shows one line saying so, which the builder writes when the file is missing. A plug-in's pages start with its band (.plugin-band). The band has the logo or initials, the name, the version and plug-in API, a status chip and How to add it.

The logo is the catalogue entry's logo, a rounded tile, shown wherever the plug-in is. A plug-in with none shows its icon or initials. The Plug-ins area may also have groups of its own articles, such as Add a plug-in. They stay in the side menu before the plug-in groups.

The home and the Plug-ins area show a card for each plug-in in the grey band design (.plugin-card). It has the logo, name and status chip, what the plug-in does, and links to Install, Settings and Problems. The Problems page starts with a flow chart, as any troubleshooting topic does.

11.8 help-centre.json

{
  "name": "Starboard", "repo": "https://github.com/TheMightyTRex/starboard",
  "site": "https://themightytrex.github.io/starboard/help/", "help_dir": "docs/help",
  "version_from": "CHANGELOG.md",
  "home": { "title": "Welcome to Starboard help", "intro": "One sentence." },
  "areas": [
    { "key": "fix", "intro": "One sentence.", "groups": [ { "key": "starboard", "label": "Starboard and set-up" } ] },
    { "key": "plugins", "none": "Snaglist has no plug-ins: it is one file you add to a page." }
  ],
  "journey": { "title": "Your server, step by step", "stages": [
    { "label": "Set up", "icon": "compass", "tone": "start",
      "cards": [ { "title": "Quick start", "line": "One command sets up everything", "to": "guides/quick-start.md" } ] } ] },
  "featured": { "chip": "New in 0.18", "title": "Update Starboard from its own page", "text": "Two sentences.",
    "to": "topics/update-starboard.md", "flow": "start: Update now\nstep: Back up your settings\n..." },
  "solver": { "start": { "question": "What is wrong?", "answers": [
    { "label": "Starboard will not open", "to": "topics/starboard-does-not-open.md" },
    { "label": "Something is wrong with an app", "next": { "question": "What does the app do?", "answers": [] } } ] } },
  "plugins": { "catalogue": "catalogue.json", "overview": "{id}/README.md", "pages": "help/plugins/{id}/{page}.md",
    "groups": [ { "label": "Network and safety", "ids": ["remote-access", "vpn-check"] } ],
    "details": { "vpn-check": { "icon": "shield-check", "status": ["Recommended", "success"] } } }
}
  • version_from names the changelog or a file holding only the version. It takes the first released version in the changelog, never Unreleased. version gives it outright, and version_link changes where the badge goes.
  • tone is start, step or fix; icon is any icon in icons.svg. A to is an article (from the help folder), a page of the help centre (solver.html, areas/plugins.html) or a full address.
  • featured takes flow (a small chart, in the format of 11.4) or image and alt.
  • With no areas, the builder keeps the older layout: the side menu is the help home's own lists.
  • footer_note: its first bold sentence (or footer_link) is the footer's only notice link, which opens a slide-out with the whole note. The footer itself matches the apps' footers, with the help centre's name and links on the left and TheMightyTRexUK on the right.
  • An area can have link_card (title, text, to, button, optional icon). This is one card at the top of its page that leads somewhere else, such as Starboard's Plug-ins area to the plug-ins' own help centre.
  • goatcounter (optional) activates visitor counting: the GoatCounter address, such as https://<code>.goatcounter.com/count. Every page loads its counter, and the footer says that visits are counted without cookies or personal data. Leave it out and nothing is counted.
  • --strict stops on an article with no group. It also stops on a page left as a placeholder ("still to be written", TODO, TBD, TBC or lorem ipsum, outside code). placeholders gives a help centre its own list of patterns.
  • The older settings still work: leave_out, sections, footer_note, banner or banners, sponsored_links, strip_between, search_example, front_door, logo_file, favicon_file.
