Content design guide

Words guide. Edit this guide on GitHub.

Version 1.6, 26 November 2026. How every word in Starboard, its plug-ins and Stallboard is written: page titles, menus, buttons, settings, messages, release notes, help and the README. It applies to new text and to anything you touch. Help articles also follow HELP-WRITING.md; changelogs also follow CHANGELOGS.md.

About this guide

What it is

This is a content design system, not only a style guide. A style guide is a set of rules written by writers, for writers. A content design system adds ready-made patterns for the moments that come up again and again: an empty page, an error, a confirmation, a setting. With a pattern to start from, anyone can make a sound decision about words without starting from a blank page.

It sits beside the component library in this repository, so words are designed with the components rather than added at the end. It has four layers:

Layer What it covers Where
Voice How every app sound Section 1
Rules Plain English, word choices, punctuation, links and statuses Sections 2 to 6
Patterns Ready-made words for common moments Section 7
Checks Accessibility in content, and the checklist Sections 8 and 9

Who it is for

Anyone who puts words in front of the people who use Starboard or Stallboard: developers writing a button or an error, designers making a board, people writing help or release notes, and AI assistants doing any of these. It is public, so you may also use it in your own projects under the guide licence (CC BY 4.0).

How to use it

  1. Read section 1 once, so you know how the apps sound.
  2. Before you write something new, find its pattern in section 7 and start from that.
  3. Keep sections 2 and 5 open while you write: the rules, and the words each app uses.
  4. Before you finish, go through the checklist in section 9.

The gallery, the tools and the other guides are listed in Design resources and tools.

Propose a change

Open an issue or a pull request on the design system repository. Name the section and the rule you want to add, change or remove, and give the words before and after. The guide is reviewed after any release that changes words in either app. How to make and test a change is in Make a change.

For AI agents: treat every rule marked Rule as a check that must pass before a change is finished. Section 9 is the checklist. When a rule and an on-screen label disagree, copy the label exactly (rule 2.11). To get an AI assistant to write instructions, and to check what it writes, follow HELP-WRITING.md section 9.

1. Voice and tone

Every app is calm and capable. Starboard cares for the server like a good caretaker: it tells you what is running, what needs attention, what it is doing about it, and what you can do next.

Always Never
Plain and direct: the answer first Clever, jokey or chatty
Calm: nothing is urgent unless it is Alarming, shouty or pushy
Helpful: every problem comes with a next step Blaming the person ("you entered the wrong…")
Honest: say when the app is unsure or something failed Vague ("something went wrong")

Assume the reader arrived mid-task and wants to get back to it. Give exactly what is needed.

2. Rules

2.1 Rule: no em dashes. Use a comma, a colon or a new sentence. A spaced en dash only for a range ("1 – 5 books" is still better as "1 to 5 books").

2.2 Rule: no contractions. "cannot", "do not", "it is", "you will". Never "can't", "won't", "it's". This includes headings: "What is new", not "What's new".

2.3 Rule: British English. organise, colour, catalogue, licence (noun), license (verb), centre, favourite, behaviour, travelled, enquiry, programme.

2.4 Rule: sentence case everywhere, including menus, tabs, chips, buttons, headings and page titles: "Manage apps", "Add missing books", "Install apps". Product and service names keep their capitals: Starboard, Audiobookshelf, Jellyfin, Docker, Hardcover, Prowlarr, qBittorrent, SABnzbd, MyAnonamouse, Audible.

2.5 Rule: "select", not "click" or "tap". "Select Save." For navigation: "Go to Settings, then Sources."

2.6 Rule: no "see" or "look". Make the subject act: "A message appears", not "You will see a message". "The results appear below the search", not "see the results". "to show today's downloads", not "to see". "Find", not "look for". A cross-reference is "Read Add an app", with the link text naming the article.

2.7 Rule: no positional directions. Never "top right", "the menu on the left", "scroll down", "shown above", "listed below" or "the table below". Name the control, the section or the item: "Select Display in the header." Positions change between layouts, phone and desktop, and they mean nothing to a screen reader user.

2.8 Rule: "page", not "screen".

2.9 Rule: "turn on" and "turn off". "Turn on Upgrade automatically", "Turn off alerts", "Turn off all plug-ins", "Save and turn on". In a sentence, say a setting "is on" or "is off", or "turned on" when something did it ("while it is on", "until you turn it on"). A status chip says "Active" or "Inactive" (section 6). Never "activate", "deactivate", "activation", "enable", "disable" or "toggle" (owner decision, 28 November 2026: the first two read as technical and are easy to confuse with each other). Where the word meant a licence key, an account or an email, say what happens in plain English: "Use this key", "Confirm your email". State the result: "Active: Starboard backs up your settings and keeps the defaults."

2.10 Rule: no exclamation marks and no emoji.

2.11 Rule: on-screen labels beat every rule. When text refers to a control, copy its label exactly and put it in bold inside steps: "Select Add missing books." Outside a step, put the label in single quotation marks: "the 'Upgrade automatically' setting". Double quotation marks are for words people say or type. If a label breaks a rule, fix the label in the app rather than misquoting it.

2.12 Rule: short sentences, one idea each. The average sentence is 15 to 20 words, and no sentence is longer than 25. Paragraphs have two to four sentences. Use the active voice and the present tense, and name who does the action: "Starboard keeps the file", not "The file is kept". Address the reader as "you". The organisation behind the app may say "we" ("We could not reach Hardcover", "We update the list each week"), and the app may also name itself ("Starboard searches again in 6 hours"). Use one of the two in a given text, not both. A passive is allowed when the doer is unknown or does not matter. Aim for about 80 to 90% active. The standard is the Plain English Campaign (plainenglish.co.uk); this guide applies it and never copies its text.

2.13 Rule: lead with the answer. The first sentence says what this is or what happened.

2.14 Rule: avoid jargon, and explain a technical word the first time on each page. Prefer the everyday word. When a technical word is the right one, add a short plain meaning: "NZB (Usenet download file)", "a daemon (a program that runs in the background)". Common ones need no explanation: ebook, URL, API. In release notes, explain it in every item, because each item is read alone.

2.15 Rule: never say how difficult something is, in either direction. Not simple, simply, easy, just, straightforward, obviously, complex, hard, difficult or advanced. Give the fact the word stood in for: a step count, a time, or what you need first. "This takes 3 steps", not "This is easy". Cut other filler words too: actually, basically, really, quite, very, absolutely, "in order to" (write "to"), "note that", "at this moment in time" (write "now") and "in the end". Prefer the plainer word: "use", not "utilise"; "start", not "commence"; "about", not "approximately"; "help", not "assist"; "buy", not "purchase". Where a word is also a label or a term in section 5 (select, enter, delete, request), the label wins.

2.16 Rule: the specific word, never a figure of speech. No metaphors. Write "layout", "order", "template" or "type", not "shape". One word per thing, and never the same word for two things (section 5).

2.17 Rule: say it once, and positively. No double negatives. Say what someone can do before what they cannot. Never describe the reader by what they cannot do. Nothing said twice in one sentence.

2.18 Rule: "enter" for typing. "Enter your API key", not "type" or "input".

2.19 Rule: the full path the first time. Name the whole route on first use: "Go to Settings, then Apps." After that, the last step alone is enough.

2.20 Rule: describe what a picture shows. Anything shown only in a screenshot is also said in words, for screen readers and for search.

2.21 Rule: never point at our own gaps. No apology for a missing help topic, an old screenshot or a feature that is not built yet. Name a limitation as a fact: "Starboard does not import ebooks from Calibre."

2.22 Rule: no promises about timing. Do not say when something will arrive, and do not mention plans.

2.23 Rule: made-up example data, and only that. Never "Test user", "John Doe", "Lorem ipsum" or real data. Use the same made-up people everywhere (Alex, Sam, Priya, Jordan). Emails end @example.com; phone numbers are 07700 900 then three digits; domains end .example. In mock-ups, the gallery and screenshots, network addresses use the documentation ranges 192.0.2.x, 198.51.100.x and 203.0.113.x. Help that teaches home network addresses may show a made-up 192.168.1.x address, because that is what the reader will find on their own network.

2.24 Rule: nest related points. When bullets explain one point, nest them under it. The parent states the fact; the children say why, how long and what to do.

2.25 Rule: use the verb, not a hidden verb. "Select", not "make a selection"; "install", not "carry out an installation"; "when you finish", not "on completion of"; "remove", not "the removal of". A verb turned into a noun adds words and hides who does what.

2.26 Rule: reading level. Aim for a Flesch-Kincaid grade of 6 or lower for text people read in the app, the help and the README. Developer pages and changelogs may reach grade 8. The grade is a guide that cannot detect jargon, so read the text as well as measuring it.

2.27 Rule: word targets are warnings. tools/verbose-check.py compares a page with a target length for its kind. A page over its target gets a warning, never a failure, because the answer may be to move content, not to cut it. A long sentence, or a paragraph of more than four sentences, is the only measure that can fail a check, and only on a line a change adds. That part is not switched on yet.

3. Punctuation, numbers and dates

  • No full stop on headings, buttons, menu items, tabs, chips, tooltips or single-line bullets.
  • Bold only for on-screen labels inside steps. No italics. Underline only for links.
  • Oxford comma in lists of three or more. Avoid semicolons: split the sentence.
  • Ellipsis only for something in progress: "Searching…".
  • Ampersand only where space is tight (a chip or a tab), never in sentences.
  • Numbers: one to nine in sentences, numerals for 10 or more. Always numerals in counts, steps, percentages, sizes and times: "3 books", "Step 2", "45%", "1.2 GB", "6 hours".
  • Dates: 1 November 2026, or 1 Nov in tight spaces. Never 01/11/26.
  • Times: 24-hour clock in the app, because times sit next to schedules: "Next search 16:31". In prose, write "at 4.30pm" only if the app never shows the same time. Write "midday" and "midnight", never "noon" or "12am".
  • Thousands with a comma: 10,000.

4. Links and buttons

  • Link text names the destination: "Open the Docker guide". Never "click here", "here", "read more", "learn more" or a bare URL.
  • A link that leaves the app says so: "Hardcover (opens in a new tab)".
  • A link to a file names its type: "Download the backup (ZIP)".
  • Buttons start with a verb and name the thing: "Search now", "Add missing books", "Delete book", "Save changes". Not "OK", "Submit" or "Yes".
  • A pair of buttons: the main action first, then "Cancel". A destructive action says what it destroys: "Delete permanently", not "Confirm".

6. Status vocabulary (Starboard)

One word and one tone for each state, in every page, chip, row, card and alert. The words come from one map in common.js (STATUS), plus the words a screen needs for its own kind of state, all listed here. Do not invent a synonym ("Online", "OK", "Offline", "Done" for Installed). A status chip is one to three words. Never a sentence. Never colour alone: every chip has its word and an icon, and the icon differs by tone (a tick, an information mark, a warning triangle, a cross, a dash, a clock).

Tones: success (all is well), info (something is happening or is on offer), warning (needs checking, nothing is broken), danger (broken or at risk), muted (off, not used, no state to report), neutral (waiting, no judgement).

7. Patterns

Use the pattern, then adapt the words. Do not invent a new layout.

Page title and heading. One main heading (h1) per page, matching the menu item and the browser tab: "Updates", tab "Updates · Starboard". Headings go h1, h2, h3 in order without skipping.

Empty state. Heading names what will appear, one sentence says how it gets there, one button starts it. - Heading: "No followed authors yet" - Body: "Add an author and Starboard gets each new book as it comes out." - Button: "Find an author" Never "No data", "Nothing here" or "This page is empty".

Loading. Name what is happening: "Loading your library…". Every spinner or bar has a text label for screen readers. A percentage only when it is real.

Error. What happened, why if it helps, what to do, then what is safe. Never an error code alone, never blame. - "We could not reach Prowlarr. Check that it is running, then select Try again. Your requests are kept." - Details (codes, responses) go in an expandable "Technical details" section. - Where the error shows: beside the thing it belongs to (a field, a row, a connection), as a danger notice at the top of the page, or, when nothing on the page belongs to it, as an error toast. An error toast stays until it is closed and can carry one action ("Try again"). Never pass a raw error message to a toast: write what happened and what to do, and put the raw message in "Technical details" or the log.

Toasts. Four kinds: success, information, warning and error. A warning says what may go wrong and what to do: "Disk 3 is 92% full". An error follows the error pattern.

Confirmation (toast). Past tense, specific, short, no full stop: "Follow saved", "3 books added to Wanted". An optional line of detail: "New books by Mara Ellison come in by themselves". One optional next step: "Undo" or "Open Activity". No celebration for routine actions.

Confirmation on a settings page (saved note). No toast: the words appear beside the button that saved, "Saved at 16:31", and stay until the next change.

Find all wanted. A button that asks a download app to search for everything wanted on a list, in four steps (owner, 26 November 2026). The same words on every Library tab, every Wanted page and every show or series. - Count first, on the button: "Find all wanted (23)". With nothing wanted, the button is inactive and says "Nothing is wanted". - Question: the title says how many ("Find 19 wanted episodes?"). The body says what happens in a sentence ("Reelix searches its sites for each wanted episode, a few at a time, and sends the best match it finds to your download app.") and three short lines: how many are wanted and how many were left out because they were searched in the last day; how fast ("Starboard asks for 10 at a time, with one minute between groups. This takes about 12 minutes."); and that you can stop at any time and leave the page. The main button repeats the number ("Find 19 wanted episodes"). Cancel is second. - Progress: a panel under the page heading with words and a progress bar that has its own label ("Searched 8 of 19 wanted episodes. The next group starts in 40 seconds."), and Stop. The words change after each group, never more often, so a screen reader hears one update a minute. While Download hours pause downloads it says so and waits. - Summary: a notice that stays until it is closed, not a toast ("Searched 19 wanted episodes. Reelix sends what it finds to your download app, and keeps searching while you follow them. 12 are still wanted."), with Open Downloads. A stopped run says "Stopped after 8 of 19" and how many were not searched. A failed run says what happened and "Nothing else changed". - Limits, so a list cannot flood the sites: 10 searches in a group, one group a minute, at most 100 in a run (the question says how many wait for the next), nothing searched in the last 24 hours, one run at a time, 15 minutes between runs. A show, series or artist page uses the same verb for its own wanted items: "Find wanted episodes (7)".

Destructive dialog. Title asks the question with the object: "Delete The Glass Harbour?". The body says what is removed, what is kept and how to get it back. The button names the action: "Delete book". Entering DELETE to confirm is only for permanent deletion.

Setting. Label in sentence case, then one line saying what changes: "Upgrade automatically. Starboard backs up your settings and keeps the defaults for anything new." Defaults are stated when they matter.

Help text and tooltips. One sentence. Not a duplicate of the label.

Callout. One colour means one thing, and the first word says it, so the meaning never rests on colour (.notice, gallery: Callouts).

Lead word Tone Use it for
Before you begin: Info (blue) What must be true or to hand first
Limitation: Warning Something that changes the outcome
Warning: Danger Something that cannot be undone
Tip: Success An optional better way
Example: Muted An aside the reader may skip

No steps and no emoji inside a callout.

Time ago. "Updated 10 seconds ago" on screen, in a <time> element whose datetime holds the exact time, with the exact date and time in words for screen readers.

A change from a date. A muted tag under the title: "From 1 December 2026". Only for a change that has an agreed date and has not happened yet. Leave it off once the change is live.

Release notes and upgrade wizard. Group as Added, Changed, Deprecated, Removed, Fixed and Security, in that order, leaving out any type with nothing in it: the same types as the changelog (CHANGELOGS.md). Each item: - a bold headline that says what the person can now do, in sentence case, with no marketing words ("seamlessly", "easily", "enhanced"); - one or two sentences on what changed and who it helps; - "Find out more in " when a topic exists. Write for the person using the app, not the developer. Never compare with before ("you used to…"): describe the new state. Database changes and the way back go in their own section.

Security in release notes. Rule: every release with a security fix has a Security section, after Fixed, that lists every security fix. Each fix is one bold title and one sentence, written so a person can judge it: what could happen, to whom, and what happens now. No codes alone, no "various security improvements", and nothing that helps someone attack an older version they cannot yet update: name the problem, not the exact request. - "Sign-in. The password is never put in the page address, even if Enter is pressed before the page has loaded." - "Saved keys. A saved API key goes only to the server it was saved for, never to a new address or across a redirect." The last item names the checks the release passed and where the results are: "Checks. This release passed Trivy, OWASP ZAP, Gitleaks, Bandit, ShellCheck and Hadolint. The results are on the security page." The checks themselves are in SECURITY-CHECKS.md.

What is new (announcement). For a release's headline feature, in the release notes slide-out or the upgrade guide. - A new feature: the benefit as the title (under nine words), one sentence on what it means for the reader, one picture of it working, "What is new" with three gains, "How to start" with up to three steps (what you need first is step 1), and one button: "Open the guide: ". - An improvement: the same without the steps. "What has changed" with three gains, one line saying where to find it, and "Nothing to set up" when that is true.

Retiring something. A removal is not news. Say what is going, the date, and what to do instead, with a link to the steps. Show it as a warning notice in the app until the date passes.

8. Accessibility in content

The full checklist is in ACCESSIBILITY.md. The content half:

  • Every control has a visible text name written as an action. Icon-only buttons have an aria-label with that name ("Close summary").
  • Alt text says what the image shows: "Cover of The Glass Harbour". Decorative images use alt="". How to write every kind of text description is in ALT-TEXT.md.
  • No instruction depends on colour, shape, icon or position alone.
  • Status changes that happen without a page load (a download finishing, a search result) are announced in a live region, using the status vocabulary.
  • Never put text inside an image.

9. Checklist before a change is finished

  • [ ] No em dashes, contractions, exclamation marks or emoji
  • [ ] British spelling, sentence case, product names capitalised correctly
  • [ ] "Select", "Go to", "Enter", "page", "turn on", "turn off"; no "click", "see", "look", "enable", "disable", "activate", "deactivate", "toggle", "screen"
  • [ ] No positional directions, no "above" or "below"
  • [ ] No difficulty words, no figures of speech, no double negatives
  • [ ] Words match section 5; statuses match section 6 exactly
  • [ ] One h1, headings in order, browser tab title matches
  • [ ] Buttons start with a verb and name the object; links name the destination and the file type
  • [ ] Empty, loading and error states follow section 7; errors say what is safe
  • [ ] Average sentence 15 to 20 words and none over 25; active voice; no hidden verbs, filler or jargon; the answer comes first
  • [ ] Icon-only controls have labels; alt text written; screenshots described in words
  • [ ] Example data is made up and follows rule 2.23

tools/check_words.py checks the banned words. tools/verbose-check.py checks the sentence length, the paragraph length, the passive voice, hidden verbs, filler, jargon, reading level and word targets, and warns only (it fails on nothing yet). The rest of this list is checked by reading.
